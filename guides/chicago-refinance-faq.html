Chicago Refinance FAQ: The Questions Every Homeowner Asks

Short answer: A refinance replaces your current mortgage with a new one — to lower the rate, change the term, remove mortgage insurance, or access equity. Whether it makes sense comes down to one calculation: does the benefit outweigh the cost within the time you'll keep the loan? Below are direct answers to the questions Chicago homeowners ask most.

Kyle Gillespie, SVP of Mortgage Lending at OriginPoint (a Rate company), NMLS #223778, has guided Chicago homeowners through refinances for 20+ years.

Published July 2026 · Last reviewed September 2026

When does refinancing make sense?

When the total benefit outweighs the cost within the time you expect to keep the loan. The common triggers: a meaningfully lower rate, removing mortgage insurance you no longer need, switching from an adjustable rate to a fixed one, shortening your term to pay the home off faster, or accessing equity for a specific goal. There's no universal rule like "only refinance for a 1% drop" — the real test is the break-even math below, run against your actual plans for the home.

What's the difference between a rate/term refinance and a cash-out refinance?

A rate/term refinance replaces your loan to improve the interest rate, the term, or both — your balance stays roughly the same. A cash-out refinance replaces your loan with a larger one and hands you the difference, turning home equity into funds for renovations, consolidating higher-interest debt, or other goals. Cash-out loans are priced and underwritten somewhat differently, and how much equity you can access depends on the program and property type — Chicago condo and 2–4 unit owners have some particular considerations worth walking through.

What does a refinance cost?

Closing costs typically include lender charges, title and recording fees, an appraisal, and often prepaid items like escrow deposits. The total varies with loan size and program. You have three ways to handle them: pay upfront, roll them into the new loan balance, or take lender credits that offset costs in exchange for a somewhat higher rate. Each is legitimate; which is smartest depends on how long you'll keep the loan. A personalized Loan Estimate puts real numbers on all three.

How do I calculate my break-even point?

Divide total closing costs by monthly savings. If a refinance costs $4,000 and saves you $200 a month, you break even in 20 months — every month after that is pure savings. Keeping the home and loan well past break-even? The refinance likely makes sense. Possibly selling or refinancing again before it? It likely doesn't. This one division problem answers most refinance questions.

Will refinancing hurt my credit?

Modestly and temporarily. A refinance involves a hard credit inquiry, which typically nudges scores down a few points for a short period. The new loan replaces the old one on your report, and on-time payments keep building from there.

Do I need an appraisal to refinance?

Often, but not always. Many refinances require one to confirm value, but some borrowers and properties qualify for an appraisal waiver based on automated valuation — which saves time and money. Whether you're eligible depends on the program, your equity, and the property; it's determined when your application runs through underwriting systems, and it's one of the first things worth checking.

How long does a refinance take?

Typically a few weeks from application to closing, driven by appraisal scheduling, documentation, and title work — with well-prepared files moving faster. One quirk worth knowing: on a primary residence, federal law gives you a three-business-day right of rescission after closing before the loan actually funds. Build that into your timing expectations.

Do I really skip a mortgage payment when I refinance?

It feels that way, but no payment vanishes. Interest on your old loan is paid through the closing date, and your first new payment typically isn't due until the month after next because mortgage interest is paid in arrears. The "skipped" month is accounted for inside the closing numbers — it's a cash-flow timing effect, not a discount. Still pleasant; just not magic.

What happens to my escrow account?

Your old lender refunds whatever sits in your existing escrow account, usually within a few weeks after payoff — a check many borrowers forget is coming. Meanwhile your new loan establishes a fresh escrow account at closing. Over time the refund and the new deposits roughly offset, but the timing means you briefly fund both; plan for it.

What documents do I need?

The standard set: recent pay stubs, W-2s or tax returns (self-employed borrowers need more, and Kyle works with bank-statement documentation where traditional returns don't tell the story), bank statements, your current mortgage statement, and homeowners insurance details. You'll get a precise checklist for your situation at application — most Chicago refinance files are smaller lifts than borrowers expect.
